# How I approach a research question

## Start with a question that can fail

State the proposed answer, the alternatives and what would count against each. A question such as “Which record refers to this person?” is more useful than a request to confirm a favored family story.

## Keep the source separate from the claim

A source has an origin, a date, a context and a retrieval path. A claim has a specific meaning. Recording both makes it possible to ask whether the source actually supports that meaning rather than merely mentioning the same subject.

## Trace shared origins

Three pages copying one register entry are not three independent observations. Track the common origin before treating repeated wording as corroboration. The Evidence Ledger sample demonstrates an explicitly supplied origin-group field; it does not discover independence automatically.

## Preserve contradictions

Do not silently repair a conflicting age, timestamp, surname or event. Record it, consider plausible explanations, and identify the evidence that would distinguish them. An attractive explanation is still a hypothesis until tested.

## Make the result proportionate

Publish the narrowest conclusion the evidence supports. A configured control is not necessarily an observed outcome. A compatible paternal lineage is not, on its own, the identity of a named ancestor. A generated reconstruction is not an archival image.

## Leave a usable trail

A finished case should carry the question, source register, competing explanations, reasoning, conclusion and next decisive check. Code should include instructions, input/output examples and tests appropriate to its actual scope.

The genealogy approach is informed by the Board for Certification of Genealogists' published proof-standard framework. This is a methodological reference, not a claim of certification or that the synthetic exercise constitutes a completed real-world proof.
